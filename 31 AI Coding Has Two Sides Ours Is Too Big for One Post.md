AI Coding Has Two Sides. Ours Is Too Big for One Post.

*Originally published on LinkedIn: <https://lnkd.in/p/gpS8XrPn>*

Post 30 covered their side of AI coding: what model labs and agent builders built into the models and agents we use. It ended by promising that the next article would cover our side: how we handle requirements, context, acceptance criteria, and operating outcomes.

Before writing it, I gathered findings on using AI to develop software: who uses it and on what software, how it is applied, what the evidence shows, and where sources agree and disagree.

The findings cover more ground than one article can. AI coding runs from startups whose code is almost entirely AI-generated to COBOL modernization and code for factory machines. People work with it in different ways: pairing with an agent, handing it whole tasks, or describing an app and getting one back. And the evidence on results points in different directions.

A post on one slice would leave the rest out. So I'm publishing the findings themselves, as they are.

Read them if you want. My intention, though, is this:

1. Use AI to analyze them from different angles and cuts: by industry, role, team, way of working, or a question your team faces. The findings are broad and unranked on purpose, so you decide which cut matters.

2. Use AI to do deeper dives: pick a finding, a disagreement, or a gap. Have AI read the sources behind it, look for newer data, or research what the findings do not cover.

You know your own situation better than I do, and everyone now has AI to do both.

The findings are a snapshot as of September 30, 2026, published in full as a Markdown file you can give to your AI tool: https://github.com/young-rex/notes-on-agentic-ai/blob/main/31%20-%20Appendix%201%20Using%20AI%20to%20Develop%20Software.md

Like the rest of this series, they are licensed under CC BY 4.0: use and adapt them freely, with credit.

What's in the findings:
- Scope and method: what is covered, and a tag for each source's type
- Sections 1–2: who uses AI coding, on what software, and for which activities
- Section 3: how it is applied (models of autonomy, named practices, tool types, organizational practices, ways of working)
- Section 4: evidence on outcomes
- Sections 5–6: where sources agree, and where they disagree
- Sections 7–8: gaps and open questions
- Sources: 103, tagged by type

In Post 32, I might take my own cut: one angle from these findings, explored in depth. If your analysis turns up an angle worth sharing, I'd like to hear it.

Take the findings. Use AI to cut them your own way, and to go deeper where it matters to you.
